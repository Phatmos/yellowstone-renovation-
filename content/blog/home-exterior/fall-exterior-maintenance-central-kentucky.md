Fall exterior maintenance in Central Kentucky should start with water: clear leaves from drainage paths, confirm downspouts discharge away from the house, and look for staining or soft materials around siding, windows, doors and deck connections. Next, check the parts people use—stairs, railings, gates and entry doors—then document anything that moves, leaks or no longer closes correctly. **If you find an unstable railing, active electrical hazard, significant structural movement or a leak entering the house, restrict access and call the appropriate qualified professional.**

This checklist is designed for a careful walk-around from the ground and from safely accessible interior spaces. It is not a roof inspection, structural certification or substitute for a licensed trade where one is required.

## The 45-minute fall exterior checklist

Choose daylight after a dry spell, bring a phone or camera, and walk the same direction around the house so you do not miss an elevation. Photograph each concern twice: one wide view that shows its location and one close view that shows the condition.

| Area | What to check before winter | Call sooner when you see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gutters and drainage | Leaves, overflow marks, loose downspouts, discharge near the foundation | Active overflow, erosion, water entering the house |
| Siding and trim | Open joints, impact damage, loose panels, soft trim, peeling finishes | Bulging cladding, repeated wet spots, visible decay |
| Deck or porch | Railings, steps, surface, framing visible from grade, drainage | Movement, sagging, soft structural wood |
| Fence and gates | Leaning sections, loose posts, dragging gates, damaged hardware | A section that could fall or a gate that will not secure |
| Windows and doors | Operation, locks, glass, weatherstripping, stains, exterior transitions | Water entry, failed locks, cracked glass, soft frames |
| Walks and exterior lighting | Trip points, loose handrails, blocked fixtures, damaged covers | Exposed wiring, severe settlement, unstable rails |

Do not climb onto the roof, lean a ladder against damaged materials or probe a wall in a way that opens the weather barrier. A roofing concern should go to a qualified roofing professional; Yellowstone Renovation does not present this checklist as a roofing service.

## 1. Keep leaves and water moving away from the house

Look up from the ground for leaves at gutter outlets, dark overflow streaks and disconnected downspouts. Then look down: water should not discharge against siding, deck posts or the foundation. Low spots, eroded mulch and soil splash on the wall help show where water travels during rain.

Lexington specifically warns residents not to rake leaves into the street because they can clog storm drains and contribute to flooding and water-quality problems. Use the city's current [leaf-disposal guidance](https://www.lexingtonky.gov/living/waste-collection/leaf-disposal) for collection and disposal options, and keep nearby drains clear without placing yourself in traffic.

Gutter work is often ladder work. If you cannot reach it safely, hire a service equipped for the height. From the ground, you can still record overflow evidence and confirm where each downspout ends.

## 2. Read the siding, trim and penetrations as one system

Walk each wall and compare similar details. Look for siding that is loose, cracked or bowed; trim that is soft or separating; failed paint that exposes bare wood; and openings around pipes, vents or fixtures. Pay close attention below roof-to-wall intersections and beneath windows, where staining may reveal a water path above the visible damage.

Do not assume every gap should be filled with caulk. Some siding joints and drainage openings are intentionally detailed to release water. A useful repair starts by identifying the material and the intended water path. ## 3. Check the deck, porch and exterior stairs for movement

Clear wet leaves from walking surfaces and stair landings. During normal use, note loose railings, shifting stair treads, raised fasteners, soft boards and slippery growth. From grade, look beneath an accessible deck for sagging, damaged connectors, soft post bases or soil and mulch piled against wood.

A clean surface is not proof that the framing is sound. Composite decking also needs its railings, connections and supporting structure checked. ## 4. Inspect fences and gates before wet soil and winter wind

Open and latch every gate. A gate that drags may indicate loose hinges, a shifted post or changing grade; simply trimming the gate can hide the reason it moved. Sight along fence lines for leaning sections, damaged pickets, rusting hardware and vegetation holding moisture against the material.

For wood fencing, focus on posts at grade, fasteners and areas shaded by dense planting. For vinyl, look for cracked or displaced components. For aluminum, check loose sections and damaged coatings. ## 5. Test every exterior window and door you can safely reach

Operate and lock the windows and doors used most often. Look for cracked glass, fogging between panes, worn weatherstripping, daylight at a closed door, loose thresholds and water staining on interior trim. After rain, note exactly where moisture appears rather than covering it before the source is understood.

The U.S. Department of Energy recommends locating air leaks before deciding how to seal them and identifies weatherstripping and caulk as different tools for movable joints and fixed gaps. Its [Energy Saver guidance](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/air-sealing-your-home) is a useful starting point. Do not seal a drainage opening or apply caulk over wet, deteriorated material.

## 6. Finish with the route people use after dark

Walk from the driveway to each entry. Clear leaves from steps, confirm handrails feel secure during ordinary use, and note cracked or settled walking surfaces. Test exterior lights from indoors and look for broken fixture covers from the ground. Do not touch exposed wiring or open an exterior electrical box; those conditions belong with a qualified electrician.

## Turn the walk-around into a repair plan

Sort your photos into three groups:

1. **Safety or active water entry:** unstable guards or stairs, failed locks, broken glass, electrical hazards, and water entering occupied space.
2. **Conditions likely to worsen:** loose cladding, soft trim, failed finishes, dragging gates, deteriorated seals and drainage that keeps materials wet.
3. **Routine upkeep:** leaf removal, cleaning, lubrication and minor adjustments after the cause has been confirmed.

Get the first group evaluated promptly. For the second group, request scopes that name the damaged assembly and the repair method rather than accepting a one-line “seal exterior” allowance. Grouping related work can be efficient—for example, coordinating window flashing with siding work—but only when the proposal explains what will be removed and rebuilt.

## Fall exterior maintenance FAQs

### What should I check first during fall exterior maintenance?

Start with drainage and active leaks, then check safety items such as stairs, railings, gates, locks and walking surfaces. Cosmetic work can wait until water paths and movement are understood.

### Should I caulk every exterior gap before winter?

No. Use sealant only where the assembly calls for it. Some gaps allow drainage or movement, and caulk cannot repair decay, failed flashing or a loose component.

### Can I inspect my roof from the ground?

You can document obvious missing materials, debris or displaced components from a safe location, but a ground view is limited. Do not climb onto the roof for this checklist; ask a qualified roofing professional to evaluate concerns.
